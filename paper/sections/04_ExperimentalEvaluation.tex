\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

The benchmark supports progressively stronger claims, and the experiments must keep them separate. Accurate final maps establish reconstruction; an advantage over non-adaptive controls establishes that question choice matters; state and graph ablations test why it matters; simulator and human comparisons test whether the conclusion survives outside one proxy environment.

Empirical runs are not yet complete. We therefore state the comparisons and falsification criteria before reporting results. This section is an evaluation protocol, not evidence that the proposed construct has already been validated.

\subsection{Claims and Falsification Criteria}

We organize evaluation around four hypotheses:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em]
    \item \textbf{H1---adaptive acquisition:} under equal turn budgets, state-conditioned policies reconstruct hidden maps more accurately than fixed and random diagnostic policies.
    \item \textbf{H2---explicit state use:} removing the working map or graph access degrades either reconstruction or early-turn efficiency.
    \item \textbf{H3---simulator robustness:} comparative conclusions persist across simulator model, grounding, answer policy, and profile style.
    \item \textbf{H4---human transfer:} simulator answers preserve task-relevant human behavior closely enough that agent rankings agree with matched human-user episodes.
\end{enumerate}

Each hypothesis blocks a different over-interpretation. A low reconstruction loss does not establish H1 if a fixed script performs equally well. H1 does not establish H2 if adaptivity comes from unstructured dialogue history alone. H1 and H2 do not establish external validity if rankings reverse under another simulator or with human users. Failure of H3 or H4 would narrow the benchmark claim rather than merely lower a score.

\subsection{Paired Comparative Design}

The initial study is a construct-validation experiment in statistical learning, not a test of cross-domain generality. The unit of comparison is a reviewed episode. Policies receive the same public graph, hidden-user instance, budget, interaction rule, and scoring profile; repeated stochastic runs remain nested within episodes.

Three controls give H1 its interpretation. A fixed-question policy controls for a reasonable diagnostic script, a random policy controls for interaction volume, and a simple LLM policy tests whether generic conversational adaptation is sufficient. Structured policies add an explicit working map, graph-aware planning, or an information-gain objective. Beating a no-interaction prior shows that dialogue contains evidence; only a paired advantage over fixed and random policies shows that selecting evidence helps.

The primary analysis reports mastery distance, exact match, missing predictions, and unsupported inference at matched budgets. Final-turn performance tests reconstruction, whereas turn-indexed curves test acquisition efficiency. Per-node signed errors reveal calibration patterns hidden by an aggregate score.

\subsection{Mechanism and Validity Tests}

Working-map and graph ablations target H2. Simulator, grounding, answer-policy, and profile-style variations target H3; both absolute changes and rank reversals are reported because only the latter directly threaten comparative validity. Matched human episodes target H4 at the turn level through state fidelity and at the episode level through ranking transfer.

Graph review is a prerequisite validity check because the graph defines the scored variables. Two domain reviewers assess source support, granularity, diagnostic usefulness, and relation validity. A matched one-shot authoring comparison tests only whether the staged workflow reduces unsupported or duplicate candidates and review effort; it does not establish a uniquely correct ontology.
