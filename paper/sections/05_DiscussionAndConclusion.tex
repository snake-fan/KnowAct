\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What a \knowact Result Would Mean}

A benchmark score is evidence only relative to the explanations that the design excludes. Low reconstruction error alone is compatible with several stories: the agent inferred the user from dialogue, exploited a strong population prior, received leaked information, or guessed well. \knowact strengthens interpretation by pairing endpoint accuracy with controlled access, evidence references, and non-adaptive comparisons.

The resulting claims form a hierarchy:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em]
    \item \emph{reconstruction}: the final map matches the reviewed map;
    \item \emph{active acquisition}: adaptive policies outperform matched non-adaptive controls;
    \item \emph{state use}: working-map or graph ablations identify a mechanism for that advantage;
    \item \emph{external validity}: the advantage is robust across simulators and transfers to human interaction.
\end{enumerate}

Evidence for a lower level does not entail a higher one. In particular, accurate reconstruction is not yet functional ToM if the same result is achieved by a fixed script. Conversely, failure to transfer to humans would indict the simulator proxy, not necessarily the internal consistency of the reconstruction task.

The interaction can be viewed as active information acquisition over a structured latent state. It differs from pool-based active learning because probes are open-ended utterances whose meaning must be grounded. It differs from conventional adaptive testing because the item bank is not fixed or psychometrically calibrated. This expressivity is precisely why provenance, grounding, and proxy validation are constitutive parts of the measurement argument.

Even a fully validated result would remain diagnostic rather than task-complete. \knowact asks whether an agent can acquire a useful user model; it does not yet establish that the model improves teaching, recommendation, or collaboration. Connecting reconstruction to downstream benefit is a separate claim and a natural next benchmark layer.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}

\paragraph{The target is authored, not discovered.}
The graph and rubrics determine which distinctions count as knowledge. Expert review and source provenance make that choice defensible and inspectable, but not uniquely correct. Rankings may change under another granularity, relation set, or rubric design.

\paragraph{Simulator fidelity.}
A simulator can be perfectly consistent with a reviewed map while expressing partial knowledge unlike a person. Leakage tests establish access control, not behavioral realism. Matched human studies must test diagnostic value and ranking transfer rather than relying on naturalness alone.

\paragraph{Artifact dependence.}
The fixed graph, rubrics, maps, and simulator jointly operationalize the target construct. Their shared design choices may make the benchmark easier for some model families. Source grounding, deterministic fixtures, artifact review, simulator variation, and human validation reduce but cannot remove this dependence.

\paragraph{Simplified knowledge state.}
A static L0--L5 state cannot capture context dependence, procedural fluency, confidence, forgetting, or learning during dialogue. Freezing state makes the reconstruction target identifiable, but it also turns explanations into observations rather than teaching events. Dynamic diagnosis will require a transition model and a different causal interpretation.

\paragraph{Initial domain scope.}
The first release focuses on statistical learning and a small authored graph. It can validate the protocol but not cross-domain generality. Additional domains must vary source style, graph topology, concept granularity, and the kinds of misconceptions that can be expressed.

\paragraph{Operational claims.}
Success would demonstrate behavior compatible with structured user modeling under benchmark constraints. It would not establish human-like beliefs, consciousness, or a psychologically faithful ToM mechanism. The term functional ToM names the tested role of the representation, not its ontological status.

\subsection{Conclusion and Outlook}

Evaluating user-aware agents requires more than placing them in a conversation. The evaluator must know what user state is at stake, make evidence acquisition consequential, and distinguish accurate inference from leakage, priors, and generic interaction skill.

\knowact offers one operationalization of these requirements. It turns a reviewed knowledge map into an explicit latent target, lets agents choose natural-language probes under a budget, and exposes the link from visible evidence to working state and subsequent action. The simulator boundary and deterministic score make this loop auditable without claiming that simulation is equivalent to human interaction.

The immediate next step is not to broaden the claim but to test it: adaptive controls, mechanism ablations, simulator variation, and matched human episodes must establish which level of the evidence hierarchy \knowact can support. Only then should the benchmark be extended to dynamic learning, downstream assistance, and additional domains.
